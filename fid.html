A little curiosity goes a long way

Think it. Try it.
Make it yours.

Four small CIRC challenges. Lots of good answers. Choose one to begin, or follow your teacher's choice.

Each challenge stands on its own. You can think, say, sketch, or make your response. All four ways count.

4

Your ideas are the main supply.

Paper, a pencil, or a familiar object are optional. Imagining works too.

  • About 10-15 minutes each. Take the time you need.
  • Miss one? You can make it up later.
  • Work on your own. Sharing is optional.

01Creative design

Redesign an
everyday thing.

An ordinary object can have an extraordinary upgrade. What would make it easier or more useful?

Optional: paper and pencil. You can imagine the object and your changes instead.

  1. Choose a tiny frustration. Pick a familiar or imaginary object: a cup, bag, chair, or pencil case. Name one thing that could work better.
  2. Give it one upgrade. Sketch, describe, or picture a change. Add two details that explain how it works. No building is required.
  3. Test it in your imagination. Imagine someone using your design. What improves? What new problem might appear? Adjust one detail.
Finish check

I can explain the original problem, my change, and why I kept or improved it.

Want another challenge?

Give your design a new limit: it must fit in a pocket or be easy to find. How would you adapt it?

Think or say: explain the same problem, change, and test in your head or aloud. A drawing is optional.

02Algorithms + debugging

Debug the
doodle bot.

This robot follows directions exactly. Its goal is one circular face with two eyes and a smile inside the face.

1. Draw a large circle.2. Put two eyes below the circle.3. Put a smile inside the circle.
  1. Run the program. Draw or imagine what these three directions produce. Follow the words exactly, even when they seem odd.
  2. Find and fix the bug. Which direction stops the robot from reaching its goal? Change the fewest words you can.
  3. Test your version. Follow your corrected directions from the beginning. Add one clear instruction that gives the face a personality.
Finish check

I can name the bug, explain my fix, and show or describe a successful test.

Want another challenge?

Write or think of three directions for a different doodle. Plant one bug, then explain how to fix it yourself.

Think or say: picture the robot's drawing and explain each change. No drawing tools or computer coding app needed.

03Observation + patterns

Be a pattern
detective.

A useful pattern lets you predict what comes next. Then you can change the rule.

Try this: circle, triangle, triangle, circle, triangle, triangle, circle...

  1. Spot a repeat. Use the example above, imagine your own, or notice a pattern from where you are. No searching around the house is needed.
  2. Explain and predict. Name the repeating rule. Say, picture, or sketch the next three parts. Check each one against your rule.
  3. Change the rule. Create a new repeating pattern. Show or describe two full repeats, then explain what you changed.
Finish check

I can explain a rule, use it to predict, and describe my new pattern.

Want another challenge?

Make a pattern that grows instead of repeating, such as 1, 3, 5, 7. Explain the growth rule and predict the next three parts.

Think or say: use the shape names as words. You do not need to see, touch, arrange, or collect objects.

04Storytelling + communication

Tell a story
in three parts.

Turn a familiar or imaginary object into a character. A sock, spoon, or cloud could have a big adventure.

Optional: three small sketches or a few written lines. A spoken or imagined story works just as well.

  1. Give it a goal. Choose your character and something it wants to do. This can be completely made up.
  2. Build three moments. Start: introduce the goal. Middle: something gets in the way. End: show what the character tries and what changes.
  3. Be your own audience. Read, tell, or imagine the whole story. Add one detail that makes the middle or ending easier to understand.
Finish check

My story has a goal, a problem, and an ending. I can explain one detail I improved.

Want another challenge?

Tell the same story from a different character's point of view. What would that character notice?

Think or say: keep the same three story moments in your head or tell them aloud. No recording, partner, or personal story is required.

Before you finish

Name one thing you changed.

Think it, say it, or note it: “At first I ___. Then I changed ___ because ___.”

Keep any work you want to keep. Follow your teacher's usual directions for what to do next. This page does not collect or submit your work.

Optional teacher facilitation

These challenges are complete without a meeting. If a class does meet, use this short routine and adjust the time to your group.

  1. Choose and launch, about 1 minute. Open one activity, read its question and finish check, and remind everyone that thinking, speaking, drawing, and writing all count.
  2. Try quietly, about 5 minutes. Read the three steps together, then give students time to try. Offer another example or extra time where helpful.
  3. Invite ideas, about 3 minutes. Invite two volunteers to share or describe their thinking. Passing is welcome. Use the matching prompt below.
  4. Revise and close, about 2 minutes. Give everyone time to improve one detail. End with “At first I ___. Then I changed ___ because ___.” A quiet response counts.
  • Design: compare two improvements and the trade-off each makes.
  • Doodle bot: follow a volunteer's corrected instruction literally and ask what made it clearer.
  • Patterns: invite two rules and test a prediction for each.
  • Story: ask how one added detail helped an audience understand the change.

Students can respond silently, aloud, in writing, or with a sketch. No camera, upload, or special materials are needed.
